Buy or rent decision tool

Czech-context financial comparison · 2026 calibration

Purpose and how to use the tool

What the tool does

The tool compares the financial side of the buy versus rent scenario for the same household: buying a property with a mortgage versus renting an equivalent home while investing the cost difference each month. It shows the development of net worth across the horizon, the development of annual housing costs and their decomposition, and the proportion of the household's terminal wealth held as liquid assets versus locked in the property.

How to use it for a fair comparison

The financial comparison is only fair if the housing being compared is comparable. The property price you enter and the monthly rent you enter should refer to the same kind of home. A frequent trap is to compare the price and rent of two different flats, which would skew the results.

Experimenting with the sliders

Once the baseline comparison reflects the household's actual situation, the sliders let you test how the result responds to changes in market and other assumptions. The most influential inputs are price growth, rent growth, investment return, the mortgage rate, and the comparison horizon. Each can shift the conclusion meaningfully on its own. Comparing very different properties intentionally, for example a smaller rented flat against a larger owned one, is also possible if the household genuinely faces that trade-off, but the result should then be read as a lifestyle comparison rather than a like-for-like financial test.

Guide

Common reasoning mistakes households make when comparing buying with renting, and the limitations of the model. The mistakes are drawn from the housing literature reviewed in the thesis this tool accompanies.

Common mistakes

1. Rent is throwing money away

The mistake. Rent is treated as money lost, whereas a mortgage payment is treated as an investment. From this view, paying rent leaves nothing behind, and buying always builds something. The conclusion is that buying is obviously the better financial choice.

The correction. Renting only feels like pure consumption if you ignore what happens to the money that the buyer commits to the property. The renter keeps the equivalent of the down payment from day one and continues to keep the monthly cost difference, which can be invested. Renting is, thus, housing consumption combined with an alternative wealth-building strategy. Whether that strategy beats buying depends on the inputs. The investment-return and rent-growth sliders are where the comparison's outcome is largely determined.

Tabner (2016); Poterba (1984); Beracha & Johnson (2012).

2. Mortgage payments are equivalent to savings

The mistake. A mortgage payment is treated as forced saving, with the household believing that every monthly payment converts cash into equity. On this view, the mortgage is a savings account with the property as the balance.

The correction. Only a minority of the monthly mortgage payment actually builds equity, and that share rises slowly over the life of the loan. The rest goes to mortgage interest, repairs, payment protection insurance, administrative fees, property insurance, and real estate tax. Even at the end of the mortgage term, the equity-building share never becomes the dominant component of the buyer's housing expenditure. The lifetime totals card breaks the components apart so the proportion is visible directly.

Poterba (1984); Hendershott & Slemrod (1982); Shefrin & Thaler (1988).

3. Buying always wins in the long run

The mistake. Buying is treated as a strategy that is bound to win if the household just holds the property long enough. The household enters the decision having already chosen ownership and treats the financial comparison as a formality.

The correction. Buying does win in the baseline scenario, but only by a modest margin, and the result is conditional on the inputs and time horizon, rather than universal. Small changes in price growth, investment return, or rent growth can flip the comparison toward renting, and these reversals happen well within the range of plausible Czech market conditions. The Czech experience of property appreciation since the early 1990s is best understood as a historical condition rather than a pattern expected to continue indefinitely. The price-growth, rent-growth, and investment-return sliders are where this is most visible.

Shiller (2015); Goodman & Mayer (2018); Beracha & Johnson (2012); Lux & Sunega (2022).

4. I will save the difference if I rent

The mistake. The financial case for renting depends on the renter consistently investing the monthly cost difference between renting and buying over the entire horizon. Most households think they will do this. In practice, the discipline is hard to maintain over decades.

The correction. A mortgage is structurally protected from a discipline failure of this kind. Each monthly payment includes a contractually required principal component, and missing a payment could result in losing the home. A renter's savings account has no such protection. If the cost difference gets absorbed into general spending, the renter's terminal wealth shrinks accordingly. This is part of why the empirical literature consistently finds homeowners accumulating more wealth than renters, even where the rational financial comparison alone does not predict that result. The investment-return slider lets you see the lower bound directly: setting the return to zero shows what the renting path looks like if no investment discipline is applied.

Beracha & Johnson (2012); Herbert et al. (2013); Turner & Luea (2009); Shefrin & Thaler (1988).

5. My friends and family bought, so I should too

The mistake. The household arrives at the buying decision before performing any financial comparison, anchored to a social norm about what counts as the right form of housing. The financial comparison is then conducted, if at all, to confirm a decision that has effectively already been made.

The correction. The cultural attachment to ownership in the Czech Republic is unusually strong by European standards, and it is well documented in the post-socialist housing literature. Survey evidence shows that 83 percent of Czech adults agree that owning a home is always better than being a tenant, and 53 percent strongly agree. The literature describes this as a rationality bias in which the influence of the social norm constrains financial, pragmatic, and investment-based reasoning, and shows that the rational comparison that the user-cost framework presupposes operates only weakly in this context. The point of the tool is not to dismiss the cultural value of ownership. It is to make the financial side of the decision visible separately, so that the cultural choice and the financial choice can be made consciously, rather than collapsed into one.

Lux et al. (2017); Lux et al. (2018); Lux et al. (2020); Stephens et al. (2015).

6. Comparing the monthly mortgage to the monthly rent

The mistake. The household compares the monthly mortgage payment to the monthly rent and treats the difference as the financial answer. This single-line comparison is what most online calculators show first and what most household conversations focus on.

The correction. The monthly mortgage is one of several costs the buyer carries. Real estate tax, payment protection insurance, property insurance, building administrative fees, ongoing repairs, and the opportunity cost of the down payment all sit alongside it. The user-cost framework, which is the main academic alternative to the simple monthly comparison, was developed precisely to capture these hidden costs. The mortgage and affordability card, and the lifetime totals card break down the buyer's full cost by component, so the comparison can be made on a like-for-like basis.

Poterba (1984); Hendershott & Slemrod (1982); Himmelberg, Mayer & Sinai (2005).

Limitations

The model is built to show the financial logic of the buy-versus-rent decision under a clear set of assumptions. The factors below are deliberately simplified or excluded.

Mortgage refixation

Czech mortgages do not offer fixed rates for the full term. The interest rate is reset at the end of each fixation period, typically every three, five, or seven years, and the new rate reflects the prevailing market conditions at the reset. The model holds the mortgage interest rate constant over the entire comparison horizon. This keeps the model transparent and easy to use, but does not capture the possibility that the rate may change during the loan's term. The interest-rate sensitivity in the thesis tests a wide range of rates that approximates the realistic spread observed over the past decade, and a household concerned about rate exposure can use that range to bound the realistic outcomes.

Macroeconomic shocks

The model assumes stable inflation, price growth, and rent growth over the comparison horizon. Real markets do not behave this way. Inflation spikes, recessions, property-price corrections, and sudden interest-rate jumps all happen on timescales shorter than a typical comparison horizon. The sensitivity sliders let you test how the result responds to different steady-state assumptions, but they do not simulate shocks that occur within the comparison period. A household with income sensitive to recessions or to a specific labor market should treat the result as a baseline against which the personal risk profile needs to be assessed separately.

Repairs as a smooth percentage rather than lumpy events

The model treats repair costs as a smooth monthly fraction of property value. In practice, repairs are lumpy. A new roof, a boiler replacement, a hot-water tank, or a major plumbing failure each costs a multiple of a typical year's repair budget. The same total cost over the horizon translates very differently for cash flow and for the household's ability to invest the cost difference reliably. Households for whom a sudden repair bill would be financially difficult should treat the repair line as carrying additional uncertainty that the smooth model does not display.

How repairs are modelled

Repair costs in the model are calculated as a percentage of the current property value, not a fixed amount. As the property appreciates, the repair line grows alongside it. This reflects two ideas. The first is that maintenance spending in practice tends to scale with the value of what is being maintained: more expensive properties carry more expensive fixtures, finishes, and components. The second is that the assumed price-growth rate is conditional on the property being maintained well enough to avoid physical depreciation. A property that does not receive ongoing maintenance does not appreciate at the modelled rate. Tying the two together in a single line keeps the model simpler than separating depreciation and maintenance, while preserving the economic logic.

Static tax treatment

The model uses current Czech tax law for the mortgage interest deduction and the real estate tax formula, and assumes that an owner-occupier's terminal sale of the property is tax-free under the current holding-period and residence rules. None of these are guaranteed to remain stable over a 30 or 40-year horizon. Tax law changes can shift the comparison meaningfully in either direction. A user whose comparison horizon extends beyond the next political cycle should treat the tax-driven components of the result as carrying additional uncertainty.

Static investment-return treatment

The model uses a single nominal annual investment return for the renter's portfolio and for the buyer's post-mortgage investments. The same single rate is applied across the entire horizon. Real markets produce returns with year-to-year variation that can affect the realized portfolio value, especially for a household withdrawing or contributing in volatile periods. The model also implicitly assumes the chosen return is achieved net of any taxes on investment income or capital gains. The investment-return slider should be set to the after-tax growth rate the user realistically expects to achieve, rather than a gross market return.

Non-financial factors

The model focuses on financial comparability. Factors such as flexibility, psychological security, attachment to ownership, expected changes in household composition, and personal preferences may be highly relevant in practice yet fall outside the financial framework. The thesis discusses how these factors interact with the financial result and how a household should weigh them against the model's output.

About this tool

A financial comparison tool for the buy-versus-rent decision in Czech housing, implementing the model documented in the accompanying thesis. The tool incorporates the user cost of ownership, the opportunity cost of capital, and the invest-the-difference strategy under early-2026 Czech market conditions.
